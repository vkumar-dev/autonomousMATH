The Tail of a Martingale

We demand uniqueness in cryptography, but the martingale property often hides it in the tail.

Question. Does the martingale property guarantee that the tail $\sigma$-algebra $\mathcal{T}$ separates points of the sample space $\Omega$?

[Make the question precise]

In cryptography, we demand that a code be uniquely decodable: given the ciphertext, there must be exactly one plaintext that produced it. But in probability, we often demand that a martingale be "unique" in its tail behavior. Are these notions compatible?

Let $(\Omega, \mathcal{F}, P)$ be a probability space and $(\mathcal{F}_n)_{n \ge 0}$ a filtration. A process $(X_n)_{n \ge 0}$ is a martingale if $E[X_{n+1} \mid \mathcal{F}_n] = X_n$. The tail $\sigma$-algebra is $\mathcal{T} = \cap_{n \ge 1} \mathcal{F}_n$, the intersection of all future information. The question is: Does the martingale property force $\mathcal{T}$ to separate points of $\Omega$? In other words, if $X$ and $Y$ are two distinct points in $\Omega$, must there be a tail event $A \in \mathcal{T}$ such that $X \in A$ and $Y \notin A$?

[Work an example, a counterexample, or a special case]

Consider the simplest martingale: a sequence of i.i.d. coin flips $X_1, X_2, \dots$. Let $\Omega = \{0,1\}^\mathbb{N}$. The tail $\sigma$-algebra $\mathcal{T}$ is the $\sigma$-algebra of "eventually constant" sequences. By Kolmogorov's 0-1 Law, every tail event has probability $0$ or $1$. But does this imply separation?

No. Consider the sequence $(0,0,0,\dots)$ and $(1,1,1,\dots)$. Both are in $\mathcal{T}$. But what about the sequence $(0,1,0,1,\dots)$? It is in $\mathcal{T}$ (it's constant in the tail sense). What about $(0,1,1,1,\dots)$? It is in $\mathcal{T}$.

Here is the key: Two distinct sequences can be indistinguishable by the tail $\sigma$-algebra. Let $X$ be the "alternating" sequence $(0,1,0,1,\dots)$ and $Y$ be the "constant" sequence $(1,1,1,\dots)$. Both are in $\mathcal{T}$. Is there a tail event that separates them? Yes: $A = \{ \omega : \omega_n = 1 \text{ for all } n \ge 1 \}$. Then $X \notin A$ and $Y \in A$. But wait—what if we take the "tail" to be the limit? The limit of $X$ does not exist, but the limit of $Y$ is $1$. So the tail $\sigma$-algebra does not "separate" them in the sense of "limit".

Let's be precise: The tail $\sigma$-algebra $\mathcal{T}$ does not separate points of $\Omega$ in general. Consider the "exchangeable" sequences. If $X$ and $X'$ are two distinct sequences, there is no tail event that can "see" the difference. The martingale property is about the expectation given the past, but it does not guarantee that the future is unique.

A sharp aside: Kolmogorov's 0-1 Law says that $P(A) \in \{0,1\}$ for all $A \in \mathcal{T}$. But this is about probability, not about set-theoretic separation. If $P(A) = 1$, then $A^c$ has probability $0$. Does $P(A) = 1$ imply $A = \Omega$? No.

[What would count as an answer]

To answer "Does the martingale property guarantee that $\mathcal{T}$ separates points?", we need to ask: "Does the martingale property force the tail $\sigma$-algebra to be a separating $\sigma$-algebra?"

Answer: No. The martingale property is about the conditional expectation $E[X_{n+1} \mid \mathcal{F}_n] = X_n$. It says nothing about the tail $\sigma$-algebra's ability to distinguish sequences. In cryptography, if we encode a message $m$ into a martingale $X$, and the tail $\sigma$-algebra does not separate $m$ from $m'$, then the decryption is ambiguous.

Left on the chalkboard

The martingale property is a "local" property—it says something about the evolution of the process. The tail $\sigma$-algebra is a "global" property—it says something about the entire sequence. They are different languages. When we demand uniqueness in cryptography, we are demanding a global property. But the martingale property only gives us a local one.
