What if the game of strategy is played with infinite strategy sets, but the payoff is finite?

The game of strategy is a classic problem in game theory, but what happens when the strategy sets are infinite?

Question. What if the game of strategy is played with infinite strategy sets, but the payoff is finite?

Make the question precise

In the realm of Recursive Theory, consider a game where players choose their strategies from an infinite set, but the payoff is always a finite number. This scenario challenges traditional equilibrium concepts.

Work an example, a counterexample, or a special case

Let's illustrate with a simple example. Suppose two players, Alice and Bob, play a game where each can choose to either "cooperate" or "defect." The strategy set for each is all real numbers, representing cooperation levels or defection levels, respectively. The payoff function is given by:

        
def payoff(coop, defection):
    # Payoff function example
    if coop == defection:
        return 1
    else:
        return 0

Here, a payoff of 1 represents a win, and 0 represents a loss. This simple example shows that a strategy that always cooperates (coop = 1) or always defects (coop = -1) is a Nash equilibrium because no player can improve their payoff by unilaterally changing their strategy.

However, this example breaks down in a more complex scenario where the strategy set is the set of all real numbers, and the payoff is always a fixed finite number, challenging the notion of a pure Nash equilibrium.

What would count as an answer

What we seek is a deeper understanding of the conditions under which a finite payoff can lead to a non-trivial Nash equilibrium in an infinite strategy game. The answer lies in exploring the structure of the strategy sets and the properties of the payoff function.
